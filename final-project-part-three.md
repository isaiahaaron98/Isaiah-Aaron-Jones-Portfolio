# Changes made since Part II

In parts I and II, my central narrative was that a small number of non-profit theatre organizations bear the artistic, and mainly financial, responsibility of mounting new works before a production is considered ready for Broadway. This part of the production process, though strenuous, is crucial for the theatre ecosystem at large. However, these organizations are unable to sustain this work from earned revenue (ticket sales primarily) alone; without a substantial amount of contributed revenue, these theatre incubators can simply not operate. The data to support my case came from the 2025 Form 990s of these organizations: overall earned revenue, earned revenue from ticket sales alone, and total expenses. With my intended audience being Broadway producers, my call to action was for them to recognize that long-term sustainability for the industry means first investing in (or donating to) these non-profits. In doing so, they would ensure that the future of Broadway continues to have fresh new works that are polished for commercial production. Though the crux of my story remained the same in Pt III, my setup, conflict, and resolution were all revised in order to tell a narrative that aligned better with the realities of the industry. 

While discussing my project with an arts management professor, a major point came up in our conversation: Broadway producers, by and large, are not philanthropists, and will likely not be moved to give money without a clear path to profit. Though my original conflict surrounding non-profit theatres having funding gaps has relevance for a commercial producer, donating to said theatres was not a viable solution to lead them to. Instead, through the help of another interview with someone not in the industry, my call to action pivoted to these producers advocating for networks to be created within the League of Resident Theatres (LORT) so that member theatres from different regions would partner in mounting Broadway-bound productions. This would 1) reduce their individual production costs and 2) allow producers to get a better sense of a production’s commercial sustainability from a more national perspective. To get a better sense of how this change came about, let's break down the modifications that were made to my setup, conflict, and resolution. 

Setup: In the wireframe I created in Pt II, I opened by introducing playwright Brandon Jacobs-Jenkins and his play Purpose. By highlighting his previous accolades, I proposed that one would think his new play would be able to be produced immediately on Broadway, but that is not the case. I then went on to explain the process of any new production, and how it generally must start at a non-profit theatre to be workshopped first. In Pt III, however, I explained the production process first in order to lay a sturdier foundation of context before diving into a narrative, which I began in the “conflict”  section, i.e. the case study. 

Conflict: In the wireframe, I introduced 5 prominent non-profit theatres that have had many productions transferred to Broadway and emphasized how their box office sales only cover about 25% of their total expenses collectively. In Pt III, however, I used the musical Lempicka as a case study, examining its pre-Broadway journey, its transfer to the commercial arena, and its eventual premature closing. This provided a more specific story and gave more context into the financial reality of the Broadway industry. 

Resolution: In Parts I and II, I made it clear that the resolution would be for producers to understand that directly investing in (donating to) these non-profits would lead to a more sustainable production process for the Broadway industry overall. In Pt III, I instead introduced the League of Resident Theatres (LORT), how they are present in all major U.S. markets, and proposed a model framework for creating networks between them in order to produce Broadway-bound productions together. My call to action went from asking producers to donate, to asking them to advocate for these networks on behalf of the Broadway League. This adjustment ultimately made more sense for my intended audience, and increased their likelihood of actually participating in the call to action. 

What I learned in this process was that I shouldn’t be afraid to pivot when it is needed. By doing so, I was able to tap into a great deal of additional data to paint a fuller picture through data visualization: taking a look at the weekly grosses of Lempica, introducing the locations of LORT members, and highlighting the average yearly audience numbers and production expenses of 4 LORT members specifically. In addition, I included the crossroad many producers feel between investing in new work or deciding to go the existing IP route, i.e. jukebox musicals. Though tempting due to a built-in fanbase, jukebox musicals wind up being extremely expensive due to the licensing expenses of pre-existing music. To visualize this through data, I showed the initial investments of 3 major jukebox musicals, underscoring how they only recouped 5% after quite a while on Broadway already. Overall, with these changes I made between PT II and Pt III, I was able to tell a narrative that was clearer due to more specificity, data, and callout statistics, and make for a better journey for the intended audience. 

## The audience

As aforementioned, I identified Broadway producers as the audience for my final data story. Though that didn’t change from Parts I and II, insights from interviews were especially helpful in honing my narrative so that my audience would feel more inclined to follow through to the call to action. One of my interviewees suggested that I incorporate a specific production from a prominent non-profit theatre and its subsequent journey to Broadway. By doing so, it centers the narrative around what is actually happening in the real world of commercial theatre, which would engage Broadway producers immediately. Additionally, it was suggested that I incorporate more contextual data/information to support my narrative. By including the dilemma that producers face between producing new works and existing IP, and including financial data of 3 major jukebox musicals, I was able to provide a more detailed portrayal of the producer’s reality in the current commercial landscape. A final suggestion that came up in my interviews that works in tandem with this is the fact that I needed additional data overall (specifically from the Forms 990). By looking at the production expenses of 4 top LORT members, it juxtaposes the reality of these non-profits to their importance within the greater Broadway ecosystem. In doing so, it makes the call to action of advocating for networks to be made between LORT members to produce new works together more urgent. 

## Final design decisions

There are many great templates in Shorthand to choose from. The template I used in Pt II seemed like the right choice in the beginning, but upon examining it further, and with the knowledge I gained from my interviews, I realized that it felt more like a slide show and not a full narrative. The shorthand template I landed on for Pt III felt more editorial, almost like The New York Times, which automatically lent itself to a more narrative structure. Furthermore, since my intended audience is producers who are likely frequent readers of publications with robust arts and entertainment sections, this template would have a familial aspect to it and could make them more receptive to the information being addressed. 

Most of the photos I use throughout the Shorthand story have an assortment of both warm and cool tones in them. While making my graphs in Tableau, I wanted to tie in these tones while also remaining cognizant of data visualization best practices. I knew orange (warm) and blue (cool) together is a fantastic combination that is accessible for those with visual impairments, so I used those colors as the basis for my visualizations. 

For the Lempicka grosses graph, I went with a standard line graph because it makes clear the grosses made each week of the 9 week Broadway run. The three bar charts I created (1 horizontal and the other 2 vertical) made the most sense for the data I was using because I was comparing numbers (be they financial or audience members) to their corresponding qualitative counterparts. Finally, the map I used to show where LORT theatres are located in the U.S. made it so that the audience could quickly see what regions/major markets are represented (I made the background dark to provide a greater contrast to the dots). Overall, what I learned is that in many cases, simple is better. There were times where I felt tempted to overcomplicate my visualizations just to make them look more "interesting," but I realized that what I created already made apparent the information I wanted conveyed, so there really was no need to overcomplicate them. This, in turn, helped me round out my data story by keeping everything clean, succinct, and with enough white space so as to not distract my audience.

# Final thoughts

Overall, I thoroughly appreciated creating this project. Many people deem themselves theatre or Broadway fans, but few know the “business” side of it. The production process is incredibly expensive and lengthy, and right now, it is not a sustainable model for the artform or the industry for both the non-profit and commercial side. My proposal is a potential way to alleviate financial stress and streamline the production process so that Broadway can continue to be a dominant force in the greater entertainment industry. Though there were times that I felt out of place using Tableau or Shorthand, I was able to push through to create a story that I am proud of. In the same vein, I was excited to learn about and use these platforms, as I know these skills will be incredibly useful in the workforce post graduation. It would’ve been great to dive into the royalties that non-profits receive once a show they workshop makes it to Broadway, but for the scope of this project, it made sense to not explore that. However, I hope to revisit this project and continue to add to it as I enter the world as an arts manager.
